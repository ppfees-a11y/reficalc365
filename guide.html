Refinancing a mortgage: a practical decision guide

A refinance replaces your existing mortgage with a new loan. It can change your interest rate, term, payment structure and amount borrowed. Evaluate the entire remaining cost, not just the advertised payment.

1. Define the goal before comparing rates

Common reasons to refinance include reducing the interest rate, shortening the repayment term, replacing an adjustable-rate mortgage, removing a co-borrower when eligible, or accessing equity through a cash-out loan. Each goal has a different success measure.

If your goal is monthly cash-flow relief, compare the monthly principal-and-interest change with the closing costs and any longer repayment period. If your goal is lifetime savings, focus on remaining total interest and all upfront fees.

2. Use remaining mortgage details, not the original loan

Your original 30-year loan may have only 18 years left. Comparing a new 30-year mortgage with the original 30-year term exaggerates the benefit of refinancing. Use the current outstanding principal, the existing rate and actual months remaining.

A statement balance is usually a useful starting point, but the exact payoff quote may include daily interest and fees. Use a lender payoff quote before making a final decision.

3. Compare the Loan Estimate, not only the quoted rate

The interest rate controls the scheduled interest calculation. APR can reflect certain finance charges and helps compare credit costs, but it is not the same as the interest rate used for your monthly principal-and-interest payment.

Ask for an itemized Loan Estimate. Look at lender charges, points, title and settlement charges, recording charges, prepaid interest, insurance and escrow funding. Some amounts are costs of the loan; others are prepayments or account funding that should be treated separately.

The lowest rate may require points. A slightly higher rate with lower upfront charges can be more economical when you expect to move or refinance again relatively soon.

4. Understand break-even and its limitations

A simple break-even estimate divides closing costs by monthly payment savings. If costs are $4,800 and the payment drops $160, the simple break-even is 30 months. That does not mean the refinance is automatically better after month 30.

If the new term is longer, some of the lower payment is caused by slower principal repayment. Compare remaining principal balances and total interest over your expected holding period, and consider the opportunity cost of upfront cash.

5. Cash-out and equity are different from savings

A cash-out refinance increases the new loan to access home equity. The extra funds are borrowed, not a financial gain, and the higher principal generally increases interest expense.

Loan-to-value ratios, credit, income, property type and loan-program rules affect eligibility. An estimate of equity is not a promise that you can borrow that amount.

6. Think about risks and alternatives

Refinancing may restart the repayment clock, increase secured debt, require a new appraisal, change mortgage-insurance requirements or carry prepayment restrictions. A home-equity loan or line of credit is a different product with its own risks.

Check whether your existing mortgage has any prepayment penalty and whether your new loan is fixed or adjustable. Compare written offers on the same day when possible, because market pricing changes.

A decision checklist

Confirm your current balance, rate, remaining term and payment. Collect written offers with rates, APRs, points, closing costs and terms. Compare monthly cash flow, expected holding period, total remaining interest and equity effects. Review final disclosures carefully before signing.
